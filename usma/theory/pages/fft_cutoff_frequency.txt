# FFT Cutoff Frequency

**Parameter:** `fft_cutoff_frequency` (FRF) / `psd_fft_cutoff_frequency` (PSD)
**Default:** `0.07`
**Range:** `0.02 – 0.25`

## What it is

A normalised spatial frequency threshold, ranging from 0 to 0.5 (Nyquist).

The signal is **not sampled in time** — it's sampled in pixels horizontally
across the ROI. So this is a **spatial frequency** in units of cycles/pixel,
**not Hz**.

## What it does

Splits the FFT spectrum into "low-frequency" (≤ cutoff) and "high-frequency"
(> cutoff) halves:

```
cutoff_indices   = np.where(xf >= fft_cutoff)[0]
high_freq_energy = np.sum(fft_mags[cutoff_indices[0]:] ** 2)
energy_ratio     = high_freq_energy / total_energy
```

The `energy_ratio` is then compared against `fft_energy_ratio_threshold`.

## Intuition

- A **clean, smooth** FRF/PSD signal changes slowly across the ROI → most
  energy sits at low spatial frequencies.
- A **noisy or double-hit** signal has rapid oscillations → elevated
  high-frequency energy.

The cutoff defines the boundary between "slowly varying" and "rapidly
oscillating".

## Effect of the default (0.07)

0.07 means anything above 7% of Nyquist is considered "high frequency". With
a typical 500-pixel-wide ROI, this corresponds to features that repeat every
~14 pixels or less — a reasonable proxy for "visible ripples".

## Effect of changing it

- **Lower cutoff** → more of the spectrum is labelled "high frequency" →
  higher energy ratios → **easier to flag as BAD**.
- **Higher cutoff** → stricter definition of "high frequency" → lower energy
  ratios → **harder to flag**.

## Calibration

Only the `PercentileBoundaryEstimator` sweeps this parameter. It tests
candidates over `[0.02, 0.25]` in steps of 0.005 and picks the cutoff that
maximises mean separation between the Good and Bad populations' energy ratios.
Neither the Bayesian nor the ROC estimators attempt to re-tune it.
